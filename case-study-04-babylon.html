TL;DR

The Azure Purview policy builder was a zero-to-one problem: no product yet, no established pattern for pairing data cataloging with active policy enforcement, and a small founding team figuring out what “govern your data” should even look like.

I led policy management and compliance UX. The people who'd own these policies understood their data deeply but weren't legal specialists, so ensuring clarity was the main design challenge.

I built a guided policy builder that reads like a sentence, made from constrained choices instead of free text, so a policy could be authored correctly and understood at a glance. Labels went through several rounds of user-tested renaming before the language actually landed.

The feature launched to a strong reception — an estimated 23% increase in product engagement — and became the foundation the rest of Purview's compliance tooling was built on.

The Problem

As Azure Purview matured, cataloging alone stopped being enough. Users could finally find and understand their data estate — but they still had no easy way to act on what they found. The design challenge shifted from helping people see their data to helping them govern it, without requiring them to become data governance specialists to do so.

No single Microsoft product could yet answer questions like “Is our data use compliant with GDPR?”, “Who accessed sensitive data and what happened?”, or “How do we prevent improper data sharing?” — and no established UX pattern existed for a platform that combined cataloging with active policy enforcement. Project Babylon was the answer.

Research

The team started with a user deep dive with customers to understand key “Jobs to Be Done” narratives for our persona across Purview. Throughout our broader customer research, it quickly became clear these three pain points are the ones we absolutely need to solve for.

  • Data catalog governance is an overwhelming effort
  • Policy creation is high impact, with potentially big consequences
  • Our persona is already doing a lot; often 1 or a just a handful of people responsible.

Insight

Our key persona the Data Officers were well versed in data; but they weren't legal specialists. Holding responsibility for GDPR or CCPA compliance is different from being trained in GDPR or CCPA itself. That gap — real governance ownership without regulatory expertise — is what made simplicity and comprehensibility non-negotiable. A data officer needed to be able to write a policy correctly, and see plainly what it would actually do, without needing a law degree to do either.

Decisions

Rather than exposing the underlying technical config directly, I designed a guided policy builder that reads like a sentence — but is composed from structured, guided selections, not free text. “Take this Effect for this Action on these Data Resources to these Subjects” — every word a discrete, constrained choice, so a policy could be authored and understood at a glance without requiring engineering or legal training to get right.

Outcome

The broader focus throughout was precise, high-scrutiny content design: every label and word in the policy builder had to carry exact technical meaning while staying readable to someone who wasn't a specialist. One clear example of that bar in practice — the fields now labeled “Data Resources” and “Subjects” went through several rounds of renaming after user testing.

Wording that read as perfectly clear during design turned out to confuse real users, partly because the industry's own governance terminology was shifting at the time.

Impact

The compliance and policy narrative I designed was very well received, resulting in a significant product engagement increase, estimated around 23% (based on memory of internal telemetry metrics); it was the feature customers were waiting for. It eventually became the foundation Purview compliance functionality grew on — eventually evolving into the broader Microsoft Purview suite that now underpins the company's entire data security portfolio.
